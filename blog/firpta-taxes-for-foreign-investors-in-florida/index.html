FIRPTA Taxes for Foreign Investors in Florida

Understand FIRPTA withholding when a foreign person sells U.S. real estate, how closing typically works and why tax planning matters before a Florida sale.

For international owners of South Florida real estate, understanding U.S. tax rules is an important part of the investment cycle. One of the most relevant rules at the time of sale is FIRPTA, the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act. FIRPTA can require withholding when a foreign person disposes of a U.S. real-property interest.

What is FIRPTA?

FIRPTA is a federal tax regime that generally treats gain from the sale of U.S. real property by a foreign person as income connected with the United States. To help collect the potential tax, the law can require the buyer or closing agent to withhold part of the amount realized and send it to the IRS.

The important distinction is that withholding is not necessarily the final tax. The seller later files the appropriate U.S. tax return, calculates the actual taxable gain and may owe additional tax or qualify for a refund depending on the facts.

How much can be withheld?

The general FIRPTA withholding rate is commonly discussed as a percentage of the amount realized on the sale, not simply the seller's profit. Certain residential transactions may qualify for reduced or zero withholding when statutory requirements are met.

Because thresholds, exceptions and entity rules matter, sellers should confirm the current treatment with a CPA or tax attorney before relying on a percentage quoted in a general article.

What happens at closing?

In a typical Florida transaction involving a foreign seller, the title company or closing attorney coordinates FIRPTA documentation. Forms such as IRS Forms 8288 and 8288-A may be used to report and transmit the withholding.

The seller should alert the closing team early that foreign-seller rules may apply. Waiting until the final days before closing can make it harder to resolve documentation or apply for a reduced withholding amount.

Withholding certificates and Form 8288-B

In some circumstances, a seller may request an IRS withholding certificate when the standard withholding would materially exceed the expected tax. Form 8288-B is commonly associated with these requests.

Timing is important. The application should be planned with a qualified tax professional and coordinated with the closing agent because the handling of funds can differ while the IRS request is pending.

How cost basis affects the final tax

Although FIRPTA withholding may be based on the amount realized, the final federal tax calculation generally depends on taxable gain. For that reason, owners should retain documentation that can support the property's adjusted basis, including the original acquisition cost and qualifying capital improvements.

  • Original purchase and closing statements.
  • Invoices for qualifying renovations or capital improvements.
  • Records of certain acquisition and disposition costs.
  • Prior depreciation schedules if the property was rented.

Does owning through an LLC eliminate FIRPTA?

Not automatically. U.S. entities can be classified in different ways for federal tax purposes, and a single-member LLC may be disregarded for some tax rules. The legal title shown in public records does not by itself determine the tax result. Foreign investors should obtain entity-specific advice before buying or selling.

Checklist for foreign sellers

  1. Tell the real estate and title teams that the seller may be a foreign person.
  2. Engage a tax professional familiar with FIRPTA and international clients.
  3. Confirm the seller's U.S. tax identification and filing requirements.
  4. Organize purchase, improvement and depreciation records.
  5. Determine whether a withholding-certificate application is appropriate.
  6. Plan for the post-closing U.S. tax return and any refund claim.

Plan FIRPTA before the property is under contract

FIRPTA should not be viewed as a reason to avoid U.S. real estate. It is a compliance issue that can usually be managed with preparation. Inversiones Davar can coordinate the real estate side of the sale while a qualified CPA or tax attorney handles the tax analysis and filings.
